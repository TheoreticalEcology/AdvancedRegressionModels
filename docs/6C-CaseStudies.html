Appendix C — Case studies

General strategy for analysis (see also the workflow in Chapter 12):

  1. Define formula via scientific questions + confounders.
  2. Define type of GLM (lm, logistic, Poisson).
  3. Blocks in data -> Random effects, start with random intercept.

Fit this base model, then do residual checks for

  • Wrong functional form -> Change fitted function.
  • Wrong distribution-> Transformation or GLM adjustment.
  • (Over)dispersion -> Variable dispersion GLM.
  • Heteroskedasticity -> Model dispersion.
  • Zero-inflation -> Add ZIP term.
  • …

And adjust the model accordingly.

C.1 Hurricanes

In Jung et al. (2014), the authors claim that “Female hurricanes are deadlier than male hurricanes”.

Specifically, they analyze the number of hurricane fatalities, and claim that there is an effect of the femininity of the name on the number of fatalities, correcting for several possible confounders. They interpret the result as causal (including mediators), claiming that giving only male names to hurricanes would considerably reduce death toll.

OK, this means we can replicate the results of the paper, even if concentrating the entire analysis exclusive on high NDAM seems a bit cherry-picking. Choosing the evaluation point after looking at the data inflates the false-positive risk, and at the maximum NDAM the estimate rests on very few storms. Another way to phrase the result is that we don’t find a main effect of MasFem. However, to be fair: the current results do say that there is a significant difference at high NDAM, and such a difference, if it existed, would be important.

What if we would have fit our own model? First of all, note that if hurricane names were given randomly, we wouldn’t have to worry about confounders (Section 7.2.1). However, this is not the case, hurricanes were only named randomly after 1978 or so.

plot(MasFem ~ Year, data = hurricanes)

So, we could either take the earlier data out, which would remove half of our data, or we have to worry about confounding with variables that change over time. The most obvious thing would be to take time itself (Year) in the model, to correct for temporal confounding.

Do we need other variables that are not confounders? There are two reasons to add them (the first is the idea of precision variables, Tip 8.1):

  • they have strong effects on the response - not adding them could lead to residual problems and increase residual variance, which increases uncertainties and cost power
  • we want to fit interactions.

I added NDAM to the model, because we saw earlier that it has a strong effect. I think it’s not unreasonable to check for an interaction as well.

As we have several observations per year, a conservative approach would be to add a RE on year. Note that we use year both as a fixed effect (to remove temporal trends) and a random intercept, which is perfectly fine, however.

C.2 Researcher degrees of freedom — skin color and red cards

Silberzahn et al. (2018) published a “meta-analysis” in Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, where they had provided 29 teams with the same data set to answer one research question: “[W]hether soccer players with dark skin tone are more likely than those with light skin tone to receive red cards from referees”.

Spoiler: They found that the “[a]nalytic approaches varied widely across the teams, and the estimated effect sizes ranged from 0.89 to 2.93 (Mdn = 1.31) in odds-ratio units”, highlighting that different approaches in data analysis can yield significant variation in the results.

The data is in

library(EcoData)
?redCards
CautionTasks

Do a re-analysis of the data as if you were the 30th team to contribute the results to the meta-analysis. You can find the data in the ecodata package, dataset redCards.

  1. Response variable: ‘redCards’ (+‘yellowReds’?).
  2. primary predictors: ‘rater1’, ‘rater2’
  3. Multiple variables, potentially accounting for confounding, offsetting, grouping, … are included in the data.

The rater variable contains ratings of “two independent raters blind to the research question who, based on their profile photo, categorized players on a 5-point scale ranging from (1) very light skin to (5) very dark skin. Note that ‘rater1’ and ‘rater2’ in EcoData::redCards are already rescaled to the range 0 … 1, as described in the paper (”This variable was rescaled to be bounded by 0 (very light skin) and 1 (very dark skin) prior to the final analysis, to ensure consistency of effect sizes across the teams of analysts. The raw ratings were rescaled to 0, .25, .50, .75, and 1 to create this new scale.”)

When you’re done, have a look at the other modeling teams. Do you understand the models they fit? Note that the results are displayed in terms of odds ratios. Are your results within the range of estimates from the 29 teams in Silberzahn et al. (2018)?

TipSolution

Base model

There is no single “correct” analysis - that is the whole point of Silberzahn et al. (2018). The following is therefore not a fully worked-out solution, but one defensible base model to start from. Each row is a player-referee dyad, so red cards are a count out of the number of games the two shared; we use that as exposure via a log offset, take the mean of the two skin-tone ratings rescaled to 0-1 as the predictor, and add random effects for player and referee to respect the non-independence.

library(EcoData); library(glmmTMB); library(DHARMa)
d = redCards
d$skin  = (d$rater1 + d$rater2)/2     # mean of the two ratings (already on the 0..1 scale)
d = d[!is.na(d$skin) & d$games > 0, ]

m = glmmTMB(redCards ~ skin + (1|playerShort) + (1|refNum),
            offset = log(games), family = poisson, data = d)

The exponentiated skin coefficient is our effect in rate-ratio units (comparable to the paper’s odds ratios: red cards are rare, about 0.4% per player-referee game, and for rare events a rate ratio is close to an odds ratio). Compare it to the 0.89-2.93 range of odds ratios reported across the 29 teams. This bare model gives a rate ratio of about 1.4, which is inside the range of the 29 teams and close to their median (1.31). The study shows, however, that the “researcher degrees of freedom” (choice of exposure, random-effect structure, which rater, count vs. binomial, and - crucially - which confounders are included) can move the result substantially. From this base model, you would go on to address the slight overdispersion (Section 9.3) and to add plausible confounders (see Chapter 7; league, playing position, player skill, …) and see how much the estimate changes.

For me, it made sense to change the contrasts of the possible confounders (see Section 2.6.1) into something more easily interpretable:

  • Did the side in which the pheromone was stay constant or not (testing for a directional persistence of the ants)

  • Was the pheromone in the left or the right arm (testing for a directional preference)

dat$directionConst = ifelse(dat$Treatment %in% c("LL", "RR"), T, F)
dat$directionPhero = as.factor(ifelse(dat$Treatment %in% c("LL", "RL"), "left", "right"))

Together with the orientation of the Maze, this makes 3 possible directional confounders, and the main predictor (if the Ant went to the pheromone in the first visit).

The snails data set in the EcoData package includes observations on the distribution of freshwater snails and their infection rates ( schistosomiasis (a parasite)).

C.7 Volcano Island

CautionTasks

Analyze ?volcanoisland in the EcoData package. Perform an appropriate causal statistical analysis (see Chapter 7; including residual checks and everything) to understand the predictors that determine the value of

  • windObs (numeric)
  • lizards (1/0)
  • beetles2 (counts)
  • survivedOf20 (k out of 20)

There are various predictors, including plot, year, x, y that can be considered. Assume that all the issues that we talked about (random effects, Chapter 4; overdispersion, Section 9.3.2; zero-inflation, Section 9.3.3; spatial autocorrelation, Section 11.3) could appear in this data.

Hint: it is possible that the possibility to observe a species depends on wind, and it is possible that lizards eat beetles, so you can also consider these variables (or predictions for these variables) as predictors of the others.

Hint 2: binomial k/n data in R is specified as a response that is cbind(k, n-k), so you would write glm(cbind(survivedOf20, 20 - survivedOf20) ~ predictor, data = data, family = “binomial”)

Hint 3: lizards may be present but not observed (imperfect detection, see Hint 1). Occupancy models, which separate the true presence from the detection process, are introduced with the same data set in the chapter on occupancy models of Hartig (2024).
