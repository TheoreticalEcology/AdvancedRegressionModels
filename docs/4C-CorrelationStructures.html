11.1 General idea

Except for the random effects (Chapter 4), we have so far assumed that residual errors are independent. However, this is not always the case - we may find that residuals show autocorrelation.

Note

Correlation means that one variable correlates with another. Autocorrelation means that data points of one variable that are close to each other are more similar (positive autocorrelation, the usual case) or more different (negative autocorrelation) than expected for independent data. This implies that autocorrelation is only defined if there is a “distance relationship” between observations.

Autocorrelation can always occur if we have a distance relationship between observations. In random effects, distance is expressed by group, and all members of a group are equally correlated. Common examples of continuous distances, where the correlation decays with distance, include:

  • Spatial distance.
  • Temporal distance.
  • Phylogenetic distance.

Tip 11.1: The “autoregressive” model zoo: SAR, CAR, geostatistical, and phylogenetic models

The word “autoregressive” is used for several related, but mathematically distinct, constructions. It’s worth knowing the differences, even though the practical recipe (de-trend first, then add a correlation structure) is the same for all of them.

  • (Simultaneous) autoregressive models (SAR): the classic time-series AR(1) model, \(x_{t+1} = a \cdot x_t + \epsilon\), defines each value recursively from the previous one plus noise. This is what corAR1 / ar1() implement, and the same idea generalizes to space as spatial SAR models (\(Y = BY + \epsilon\)).

  • Conditional autoregressive models (CAR) (Besag, 1974): instead of a recursive equation, you specify the distribution of each point conditional on its neighbors, \(Y_i \mid Y_{-i} \sim N(\sum_{j \sim i} c_{ij} Y_j,\ \tau_i^2)\). This requires a discrete neighborhood structure (e.g. an adjacency matrix for areal/lattice data, as used in packages like CARBayes or INLA’s besag model) and is common in spatial epidemiology. CAR and SAR are both Markovian and closely related, but they are built differently and generally do not induce the same joint distribution.

  • Geostatistical / covariance-function models: the spatial example used later in this chapter (Section 11.3; corExp, exp() in glmmTMB) doesn’t use a discrete neighborhood at all. Instead, the correlation between any two points is a direct function of their (continuous) distance, e.g. exponential decay. This “kriging” / Gaussian-process style is a third, separate family - despite being spatial, it is technically neither SAR nor CAR.

  • Phylogenetic models (Brownian motion, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck): perhaps surprisingly, these are close relatives of the SAR family, not CAR. Brownian motion is a continuous-time random walk (the \(a \to 1\) limit of AR(1)), and the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process used for corMartins / corPagel is exactly the continuous-time analog of AR(1): its exact discrete-time solution is \(x_{t+1} = \mu + e^{-\theta \Delta t}(x_t - \mu) + \epsilon_t\), an AR(1) recursion with \(a = e^{-\theta \Delta t}\).

You don’t need to memorize this taxonomy for the course, but if you go looking specifically for “CAR models” in the literature or in packages like INLA, be aware that this refers to the Besag-style conditional construction - not to the temporal, phylogenetic, or corExp-style spatial models we actually use in this chapter.

Besag, J. (1974) “Spatial interaction and the statistical analysis of lattice systems,” Journal of the Royal Statistical Society: Series B (Methodological), 36(2), pp. 192–225. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1974.tb00999.x.

11.1.1 Models to deal with autocorrelation

If we find autocorrelation in the residuals of our model, there can be several reasons, which we can address by different structures.

Important

In the context of regression models, we are never interested in the autocorrelation of the response / predictors per se, but only in the residuals. Thus, it doesn’t make sense to assume that you need a spatial model only because you have a spatially autocorrelated signal.

  1. Autocorrelation can occur because we have a correlated misfit, i.e. there is a trend along the distance axis (time, space, or phylogeny). If this is the case, de-trending the model (with a linear regression term or a spline) will remove the residual autocorrelation. We should always de-trend first before we consider moving to a model with a residual correlation structure.

  2. Only after accounting for the trend, we should test if there is a residual spatial / temporal / phylogenetic autocorrelation. If that is the case, we would usually use a so-called autoregressive structure (see Tip 11.1 for the different flavors of these models). In these models, we make parametric assumptions for how the correlation between data points falls off with distance. When we speak about spatial / temporal / phylogenetic regressions, we usually refer to these or similar models.

11.1.2 R implementation

To de-trend, you can just use standard regression terms or splines on time or space. For the rest of this chapter, we will concentrate on how to specify “real” correlation structures. However, in the case studies, you should always de-trend first.

To account for “real” autocorrelation of residuals, similarly to the variance modeling (Section 10.1.2), we can add correlation structures

  • for normal responses in nlme::gls, see https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/nlme/html/corClasses.html
  • for GLMs using glmmTMB, see https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/glmmTMB/vignettes/covstruct.html.

The following sections (Section 11.2, Section 11.3, Section 11.4) provide examples and further comments on how to do this.

Note

Especially for spatial models, both nlme and glmmTMB are relatively slow. There are a large number of specialized packages that deal in particular with the problem of spatial models, including MASS::glmmPQL, BRMS, INLA, spaMM, and many more. Bayesian autoregressive models, and state-space models that separate process and observation error, are covered in Hartig (2024) (autoregressive models, state-space models). To keep things simple and concentrate on the principles, however, we will stick with the packages you already know.

Hartig, F. (2024) “Introduction to Bayesian statistics with R.” Available at: https://florianhartig.github.io/LearningBayes/.

11.2 Temporal correlation structures

To introduce temporal autoregressive models, let’s simulate some data first. The most simple (and common) temporal structure is the AR1 model, aka autoregressive model with lag 1. The AR1 assumes that the next data point (or residual) originates from the last data point, multiplied by a factor a (with |a| < 1), plus a normally distributed residual, in the form

\[x_{t+1} = a \cdot x_t + \epsilon\]

Let’s simulate some data according to this model

# simulate temporally autocorrelated data
AR1sim<-function(n, a){
  x = rep(NA, n)
  x[1] = 0
  for(i in 2:n){
    x[i] = a * x[i-1] + rnorm(1)
  }
  return(x)
}

set.seed(123)
obs = AR1sim(1000, 0.9)
plot(obs)

As we can see, we have a temporal correlation here. As we have not modeled / specified any further predictors, the correlation in the signal will transform directly in the correlations of the residuals if we fit a model:

fit = lm(obs~1)

We can quantify the autocorrelation by the acf function, which quantifies correlation between observations as a function of their lag (temporal distance). Note that although we modeled only a lag of 1, we will get correlations with many lags, because the correlation effect “trickles down”.

To check what the actual underlying model is, it may be useful to plot the partial correlation coefficient, i.e. the correlation at lag k that remains after accounting for lags 1 to k-1 (the last coefficient of an AR(k) model).

pacf(residuals(fit))

Here, we see that we actually only have a correlation with lag 1. You can also check for temporal correlation with the DHARMa package

Note

Remember (Section 11.1.1): in general, for spatial / temporal data, there are two processes that can create residual autocorrelation:

  1. There is a misfit trend in time (or space), which creates residual correlation.
  2. There truly is autocorrelation after accounting for the trend.

Unfortunately, the distinction between a larger trend and a correlation is quite fluid. Nevertheless, one should always first check for and remove the trend, typically by including time/space as a predictor, potentially in a flexible way (GAMs come in handy). After this is done, we can fit a model with a temporally/spatially correlated error.

NoteResidual checks conditional on the fitted random effects

glmmTMB fits the AR1 structure as a random effect, with one value per time point. When DHARMa simulates new data to calculate residuals, the question is whether these random effects are re-simulated (unconditional simulations) or kept at their fitted values (conditional simulations, see also Tip 4.3). Since DHARMa 0.5.0, simulateResiduals() simulates conditionally by default. For glmmTMB models, DHARMa does this via glmmTMB::set_simcodes(fit$obj, val = "fix"); for a few covariance structures, this is not yet possible, and DHARMa then falls back to unconditional simulations with a message. With simulateREs = "unconditional", all random effects are re-simulated. To simulate conditionally directly with glmmTMB:

glmmTMB::set_simcodes(fitGLMMTMB$obj, val = "fix") # note: this modifies the model object
simulate(fitGLMMTMB, nsim = 10)

Let’s compare the two options for our AR1 model:

resU = simulateResiduals(fitGLMMTMB, simulateREs = "unconditional")
testTemporalAutocorrelation(resU, time = 1:1000)

    Durbin-Watson test

data:  simulationOutput$scaledResiduals ~ 1
DW = 0.24923, p-value < 2.2e-16
alternative hypothesis: true autocorrelation is not 0
resC = simulateResiduals(fitGLMMTMB) # conditional (default)
testTemporalAutocorrelation(resC, time = 1:1000)

    Durbin-Watson test

data:  simulationOutput$scaledResiduals ~ 1
DW = 2.8049, p-value < 2.2e-16

The unconditional residuals still contain the full autocorrelation, because the AR1 process is re-simulated, so they cannot tell us whether the AR1 model is adequate. The conditional residuals no longer show the positive autocorrelation, but a negative one (Durbin-Watson statistic > 2): with one random effect per time point, the fitted random effects also absorb part of the independent noise, so the conditional residuals are not independent either. Therefore, use DHARMa’s autocorrelation test mainly on the model without a correlation structure, to decide whether you need one. To check the fitted AR1 model, a cleaner option for normal responses are the normalized residuals of gls, from which the estimated correlation has been removed:

acf(residuals(fitGLS, type = "normalized"))
NoteWhy de-trending is important

As discussed in Section 11.1.1, a misfit trend also creates residual autocorrelation. Let’s simulate data with a nonlinear time trend, but with independent residuals, i.e. without any autocorrelation:

set.seed(123)
time = 1:1000/100
y = time + 2*sin(time/0.4) + rnorm(1000)
data = data.frame(y = y, time = time)
plot(y ~ time, data = data)

If we only remove a linear trend, the residuals are strongly autocorrelated, because the sine wave that we did not model is still in them:

fit1 = lm(y ~ time, data = data)
res1 = simulateResiduals(fit1, plot = TRUE)
testTemporalAutocorrelation(res1, time = data$time)

    Durbin-Watson test

DW = 0.62908, p-value < 2.2e-16

The correct reaction is not to add an AR1 structure, but to de-trend properly, e.g. with a spline (Section 2.5.3):

library(mgcv)
fit2 = gam(y ~ s(time), data = data)

testTemporalAutocorrelation(res2, time = data$time)

    Durbin-Watson test

data:  simulationOutput$scaledResiduals ~ 1
DW = 1.9629, p-value = 0.5574
alternative hypothesis: true autocorrelation is not 0

After de-trending, the residuals look fine and there is no autocorrelation left, so we don’t need a correlation structure.

11.4 Phylogenetic structures (PGLS)

Closely related species share evolutionary history, so their trait values are not independent. Under Brownian motion, the covariance of two species is proportional to the length of the branch they share (vcv(tree)); a PGLS is simply a gls with this covariance structure, where the distance in the correlation structure is the phylogenetic distance. We fit with method = "ML" so that models with different fixed effects or correlation structures can be compared by AIC / LRT (Section 4.2.4).

11.5 Take-home messages

Tip
  1. Only the autocorrelation of the residuals matters, not that of the response or the predictors. Ignoring it makes p-values unreliable, so check the residuals of a model without a correlation structure (acf, variogram, DHARMa) to see whether you need one.
  2. De-trend first (e.g. with a spline), and add a correlation structure only if autocorrelation remains. A correlation structure fitted on top of a misspecified trend absorbs the misfit and wrongly suggests a random correlation.
  3. To check a fitted correlation structure, use the normalized residuals of gls (acf(residuals(fit, type = "normalized"))); DHARMa’s autocorrelation test is mainly useful for the model without correlation structure.
  4. Phylogenetic correlation can make the p-values of an lm too small or too large. Check the residuals for phylogenetic signal and use a PGLS if there is some.
  5. Do not interpret a model that has not converged; take warnings seriously and compare several starting values. Residual correlations between responses are not evidence of causal interactions.

11.6 Multivariate GLMs

In recent years, multivariate GLMs, in particular the multivariate probit model, and latent variable versions thereof, have become popular for the analysis of community data. The keyword here is “joint species distribution models” (jSDMs).

Briefly, what we want a jSDM to do is to fit a vector of responses (could be abundance or presence / absence data) as a function of environmental predictors and a covariance between the responses. The model is thus

\[y \sim f(x) + \Sigma\] where y is a response vector, f(x) is a matrix with effects, and \(\Sigma\) is a covariance matrix that we want to estimate.

You can fit these models directly in lme4 or glmmTMB via implementing an RE per species

lme4::glmer(abundance ~ 0 + species + env:species + (0 + species | site), family = poisson, data = dat)
glmmTMB(abundance ~ 0 + species + env:species + (0 + species | site), family = poisson, data = dat)

however, these so-called full-rank models have a lot of degrees of freedom and are slow to compute if the number of species gets large. It has therefore become common to fit rank-reduced versions of these models using a latent-variable reparameterization of the model above. A latent-variable version of this model can be fit in glmmTMB via this syntax

glmmTMB(abundance ~ 0 + species + env:species + rr(0 + species | site, d = 2),
        family = poisson, data = dat)

The parameter d controls the number of latent variables. With d latent variables, all correlations between the responses are generated by d hidden gradients across the sampling units (here: sites), and each response has one loading per gradient; with small d, correlations close to ±1 therefore occur by construction.

Of course, there are many more specialized packages for fitting latent-variable jSDMs in R right now, including hmsc, gllvm or sjSDM, but I find it nice to place these models within the general topic of correlations, and to realize that we can fit them with the same methods and packages as all other correlation structures. The rr() structure in glmmTMB is described by McGillycuddy et al. (2025).

McGillycuddy, M. et al. (2025) “Parsimoniously fitting large multivariate random effects in glmmTMB,” Journal of Statistical Software, 112(1). Available at: https://doi.org/10.18637/jss.v112.i01.

ImportantResidual correlation is not a causal link

A residual correlation between two responses means that they are higher (or lower) in the same units than the predictors explain. This can be caused by unmeasured conditions of the units, by spatial structure (Section 11.3), or by a direct effect of one response on the other; the model cannot tell these apart. For species, this means that co-occurrence is not evidence of interactions (Blanchet, Cazelles and Gravel, 2020).

Blanchet, F.G., Cazelles, K. and Gravel, D. (2020) “Co-occurrence is not evidence of ecological interactions,” Ecology Letters, 23(7), pp. 1050–1063. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.13525.
NoteCommunity data in practice

In ecology, data on many species are often analyzed with ordination (NMDS or PCoA with PERMANOVA, RDA, CCA), which describes the species composition as a whole but gives no model for single species. Model-based alternatives are mvabund::manyglm, gllvm (which also offers ordered and hurdle beta for cover data, as compared by Korhonen et al. (2024)), Hmsc (Bayesian) and sjSDM. If the hypothesis is about diversity, species richness may be the simpler response.
